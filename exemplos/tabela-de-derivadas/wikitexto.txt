{{Short description|Rules for computing derivatives of functions}}
{{Calculus |Differential}}
This article is a summary of '''differentiation rules''', that is, rules for computing the [[derivative]] of a [[function (mathematics)|function]] in [[calculus]].

==Elementary rules of differentiation==
Unless otherwise stated, all functions are functions of [[real number]]s (<math display="inline">\mathbb{R}</math>) that return real values, although, more generally, the formulas below apply wherever they are [[well defined]],<ref>''Calculus (5th edition)'', F. Ayres, E. Mendelson, Schaum's Outline Series, 2009, {{ISBN|978-0-07-150861-2}}.</ref><ref>''Advanced Calculus (3rd edition)'', R. Wrede, M.R. Spiegel, Schaum's Outline Series, 2010, {{ISBN|978-0-07-162366-7}}.</ref> including the case of [[complex number]]s (<math display="inline">\mathbb{C}</math>).<ref>''Complex Variables'', M.R. Spiegel, S. Lipschutz, J.J. Schiller, D. Spellman, Schaum's Outlines Series, McGraw Hill (USA), 2009, {{ISBN|978-0-07-161569-3}}</ref>

===Constant term rule===
For any value of <math display="inline">c</math>, where <math display="inline">c \in \mathbb{R}</math>, if <math display="inline">f(x)</math> is the constant function given by <math display="inline">f(x) = c</math>, then <math display="inline">\frac{df}{dx} = 0</math>.<ref>{{cite web |title=Differentiation Rules |url=https://courseware.cemc.uwaterloo.ca/11/assignments/47/6 |website=University of Waterloo – CEMC Open Courseware |access-date=3 May 2022}}</ref>

====Proof====
Let <math display="inline">c \in \mathbb{R}</math> and <math display="inline">f(x) = c</math>. By the definition of the derivative:
<math display="block">\begin{align}
f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(c) - (c)}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{0}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} 0 \\
&= 0.
\end{align}</math>

This computation shows that the derivative of any constant function is 0. 

====Intuitive (geometric) explanation====
The [[derivative]] of the function at a point is the slope of the line [[tangent]] to the curve at the point. The [[slope]] of the constant function is 0, because the [[Tangent|tangent line]] to the constant function is horizontal and its angle is 0.

In other words, the value of the constant function, <math display="inline">y</math>, will not change as the value of <math display="inline">x</math> increases or decreases.

[[File:Tangent function animation.gif|right|frame|At each point, the [[derivative]] is the slope of a [[Line (geometry)|line]] that is [[tangent]] to the [[curve]] at that point. Note: the derivative at point A is [[positive number|positive]] where green and dash–dot, [[negative number|negative]] where red and dashed, and [[0]] where black and solid.]]

===Linearity of differentiation===
{{Main|Linearity of differentiation}}
For any functions <math display="inline">f</math> and <math display="inline">g</math> and any real numbers <math display="inline">a</math> and <math display="inline">b</math>, the derivative of the function <math display="inline">h(x) = af(x) + bg(x)</math> with respect to <math display="inline">x</math> is <math display="inline">h'(x) = a f'(x) + b g'(x)</math>.

In [[Leibniz's notation]], this formula is written as:
<math display="block"> \frac{d(af+bg)}{dx}  = a\frac{df}{dx} +b\frac{dg}{dx}.</math>

Special cases include:
* The constant factor rule:
<math display="block">(af)' = af', </math>
* The sum rule:
<math display="block">(f + g)' = f' + g',</math>
* The difference rule:
<math display="block">(f - g)' = f' - g'.</math>

===Product rule===
{{Main|Product rule}}
For the functions <math display="inline">f</math> and <math display="inline">g</math>, the derivative of the function <math display="inline">h(x) = f(x) g(x)</math> with respect to <math display="inline">x</math> is:
<math display="block"> h'(x) = (fg)'(x) = f'(x) g(x) + f(x) g'(x).</math>

In Leibniz's notation, this formula is written:
<math display="block">\frac{d(fg)}{dx} = g \frac{df}{dx} + f \frac{dg}{dx}.</math>

===Chain rule===
{{Main|Chain rule}}
The derivative of the function <math display="inline">h(x) = f(g(x))</math> is:
<math display="block"> h'(x) = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x).</math>

In Leibniz's notation, this formula is written as:
<math display="block">\frac{d}{dx}h(x) = \left.\frac{d}{dz}f(z)\right|_{z=g(x)}\cdot \frac{d}{dx}g(x),</math>
often abridged to:
<math display="block">\frac{dh(x)}{dx} = \frac{df(g(x))}{dg(x)} \cdot \frac{dg(x)}{dx}.</math>

Focusing on the notion of maps, and the differential being a map <math display="inline">\text{D}</math>, this formula is written in a more concise way as:
<math display="block"> [\text{D} (f\circ g)]_x = [\text{D} f]_{g(x)} \cdot [\text{D}g]_x.</math>

===Inverse function rule===
{{Main|Inverse function rule}}
If the function <math display="inline">f</math> has an [[inverse function]] <math display="inline">g</math>, meaning that <math display="inline">g(f(x)) = x</math> and <math display="inline">f(g(y)) = y</math>, then:
<math display="block">g' = \frac{1}{f'\circ g}.</math>

In Leibniz notation, this formula is written as:
<math display="block"> \frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{1}{\frac{dy}{dx}}.</math>

==Power laws, polynomials, quotients, and reciprocals==
===Polynomial or elementary power rule===
{{Main|Power rule}}
If <math display="inline">f(x) = x^r</math>, for any real number <math display="inline">r \neq 0</math>, then:
<math display="block">f'(x) = rx^{r-1}.</math>

When <math display="inline">r = 1</math>, this formula becomes the special case that, if <math display="inline">f(x) = x</math>, then <math display="inline">f'(x) = 1</math>.

Combining the power rule with the sum and constant multiple rules permits the computation of the derivative of any polynomial.

===Reciprocal rule===
{{Main|Reciprocal rule}}
The derivative of <math display="inline">h(x)=\frac{1}{f(x)}</math> for any (nonvanishing) function <math display="inline">f</math> is:
<math display="block"> h'(x) = -\frac{f'(x)}{(f(x))^2},</math>
wherever <math display="inline">f</math> is nonzero.

In Leibniz's notation, this formula is written:
<math display="block"> \frac{d\left(\frac{1}{f}\right)}{dx} = -\frac{1}{f^2}\frac{df}{dx}.</math>

The reciprocal rule can be derived either from the quotient rule or from the combination of power rule and chain rule.

===Quotient rule===
{{Main|Quotient rule}}
If <math display="inline">f</math> and <math display="inline">g</math> are functions, then:
<math display="block">\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - g'f}{g^2},</math>
wherever <math display="inline">g</math> is nonzero.

This can be derived from the product rule and the reciprocal rule.

===Generalized power rule===
{{Main|Power rule}}
The elementary power rule generalizes considerably. The most general power rule is the '''functional power rule''': for any functions <math display="inline">f</math> and <math display="inline">g</math>,
<math display="block">(f^g)' = \left(e^{g\ln f}\right)' = f^g\left(f'{g \over f} + g'\ln f\right),\quad</math>
wherever both sides are well defined.

Special cases:
* If <math display="inline">f(x)=x^a</math>, then <math display="inline">f'(x)=ax^{a-1}</math> when <math display="inline">a</math> is any nonzero real number and <math display="inline">x</math> is positive.
* The reciprocal rule may be derived as the special case where <math display="inline">g(x)=-1\!</math>.

==Derivatives of exponential and logarithmic functions==
<math display="block"> \frac{d}{dx}\left(c^{ax}\right) = {ac^{ax} \ln c } ,\qquad c > 0.</math>
The equation above is true for all <math>c</math>, but the derivative for <math>c<0</math> yields a complex number.

<math display="block"> \frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{ax}\right) = ae^{ax}.</math>

<math display="block"> \frac{d}{dx}\left( \log_c x\right) = {1 \over x \ln c} , \qquad c > 1.</math>
The equation above is also true for all <math display="inline">c</math> but yields a complex number if <math display="inline">c<0</math>.

<math display="block"> \frac{d}{dx}\left( \ln x\right)  = {1 \over x} ,\qquad x > 0.</math>

<math display="block"> \frac{d}{dx}\left( \ln |x|\right) = {1 \over x} ,\qquad x \neq 0.</math>

<math display="block"> \frac{d}{dx}\left( W(x)\right) = {1 \over {x+e^{W(x)}}} ,\qquad x > -{1 \over e},</math>
where <math display="inline">W(x)</math> is the [[Lambert W function]].

<math display="block"> \frac{d}{dx}\left( x^x \right) = x^x(1+\ln x).</math>

<math display="block"> \frac{d}{dx}\left( f_{1}(x)^{f_{2}(x)^{\left ( ... \right )^{f_{n}(x)}}} \right ) = \left [\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\partial }{\partial x_{k}} \left( f_{1}(x_1)^{f_{2}(x_2)^{\left ( ... \right )^{f_{n}(x_n)}}} \right ) \right ] \biggr\vert_{x_1 = x_2 = ... =x_n = x},\qquad \text{ if } f_{i<n}(x) > 0 \text{ and }\frac{df_{i}}{dx} \text{ exists.}</math>

===Logarithmic derivatives===
The [[logarithmic derivative]] is another way of stating the rule for differentiating the [[logarithm]] of a function (using the chain rule):
<math display="block"> (\ln f)'= \frac{f'}{f},</math>
wherever <math display="inline">f</math> is positive.

[[Logarithmic differentiation]] is a technique which uses logarithms and its differentiation rules to simplify certain expressions before actually applying the derivative.{{citation needed|date=October 2021}}

Logarithms can be used to remove exponents, convert products into sums, and convert division into subtraction—each of which may lead to a simplified expression for taking derivatives.

==Derivatives of special functions==
===Gamma function===
{{Main|Gamma function}}
<math display="block">\Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty t^{x-1} e^{-t}\, dt</math>
<math display="block">\begin{align}
\Gamma'(x) & = \int_0^\infty t^{x-1} e^{-t} \ln t\,dt \\
& = \Gamma(x) \left(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(\ln\left(1 + \dfrac{1}{n}\right) - \dfrac{1}{x + n}\right) - \dfrac{1}{x}\right) \\
& = \Gamma(x) \psi(x),
\end{align}</math>
with <math display="inline">\psi(x)</math> being the [[digamma function]], expressed by the parenthesized expression to the right of <math display="inline">\Gamma(x)</math> in the line above.

===Riemann zeta function===
{{Main|Riemann zeta function}}
<math display="block">\zeta(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^x}</math>
<math display="block">\begin{align}
\zeta'(x) & = -\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\ln n}{n^x}
=-\frac{\ln 2}{2^x} - \frac{\ln 3}{3^x} - \frac{\ln 4}{4^x} - \cdots \\
& = -\sum_{p \text{ prime}} \frac{p^{-x} \ln p}{(1-p^{-x})^2} \prod_{q \text{ prime}, q \neq p} \frac{1}{1-q^{-x}}
\end{align}</math>

==Derivatives of integrals==
{{Main|Differentiation under the integral sign}}
Suppose that it is required to differentiate with respect to <math display="inline">x</math> the function:
<math display="block">F(x)=\int_{a(x)}^{b(x)}f(x,t)\,dt,</math>

where the functions <math display="inline">f(x,t)</math> and <math display="inline">\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,f(x,t)</math> are both continuous in both <math display="inline">t</math> and <math display="inline">x</math> in some region of the <math display="inline">(t,x)</math> plane, including <math display="inline">a(x)\leq t\leq b(x)</math>, where <math display="inline">x_0\leq x\leq x_1</math>, and the functions <math display="inline">a(x)</math> and <math display="inline">b(x)</math> are both continuous and both have continuous derivatives for <math display="inline">x_0\leq x\leq x_1</math>. Then, for <math display="inline">\,x_0\leq x\leq x_1</math>:
<math display="block"> F'(x) = f(x,b(x))\,b'(x) - f(x,a(x))\,a'(x) + \int_{a(x)}^{b(x)} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\, f(x,t)\; dt\,. </math>

This formula is the general form of the [[Leibniz integral rule]] and can be derived using the [[fundamental theorem of calculus]].

==Derivatives to ''n''th order==
Some rules exist for computing the <math display="inline">n</math>th derivative of functions, where <math display="inline">n</math> is a positive integer, including:

===Faà di Bruno's formula===
{{Main|Faà di Bruno's formula}}
If <math display="inline">f</math> and <math display="inline">g</math> are <math display="inline">n</math>-times differentiable, then:
<math display="block">  \frac{d^n}{d x^n} [f(g(x))]= n! \sum_{\{k_m\}} f^{(r)}(g(x)) \prod_{m=1}^n \frac{1}{k_m!} \left(g^{(m)}(x) \right)^{k_m},</math>
where <math display="inline"> r = \sum_{m=1}^{n-1} k_m</math> and the set <math display="inline"> \{k_m\}</math> consists of all non-negative integer solutions of the [[Diophantine equation]] <math display="inline"> \sum_{m=1}^{n} m k_m = n</math>.

===General Leibniz rule===
{{Main|General Leibniz rule}}
If <math display="inline">f</math> and <math display="inline">g</math> are <math display="inline">n</math>-times differentiable, then:
<math display="block"> \frac{d^n}{dx^n}[f(x)g(x)] = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} \frac{d^{n-k}}{d x^{n-k}} f(x) \frac{d^k}{d x^k} g(x).</math>
