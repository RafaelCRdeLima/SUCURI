Differential geometry through exercises. This tutorial works through 85 exercises from problem sets used in courses on general relativity and Riemannian geometry — Cambridge, MIT, Caltech, Berlin, Oregon, Nagoya, Utrecht, Leiden —, grouped by the notation they are naturally stated in: index notation, with components, indices and the Einstein summation; and index-free, with vector fields, connections and forms. For each one, what Sucuri does with it: solves it, solves it partly, or does not — and why.

Every exercise solved here is run by the test suite on every change to the program: the printed output is the output. Before each solution comes the full problem statement, quoted from the original with the exact reference and link, and a note on what Sucuri does with it.

How to read

Each exercise gives the source, the problem statement in brief, and the cells that solve it in the notebook, with the answer. Three badges:

solves Sucuri reaches the result of the exercise — by computation, or by prove, which returns the checked chain of steps.
partly it solves one part, or the deciding step, or only with a hypothesis that the exercise asks you to derive.
not solved the exercise asks for something Sucuri cannot yet write or derive — and section V says what.

Two things hold for everything that follows. Declaring decides the reading: an index is only an index, a vector only a vector, once declared — the manual explains why. And prove uses only the named hypotheses: what the engine knows on its own is what holds in any textbook (linearity, Leibniz, antisymmetry of the bracket, d² = 0); the rest, including every sign convention, goes in as a written hypothesis.

Index-free notation went further, and not by chance: that was where Sucuri had a proof engine; now index notation has its own. Two exercises stated in indices (9 and 11) only come out because they were rewritten without indices — which is, after all, what abstract notation exists to allow. In index notation, going from ∇ to Γ and from Γ to ∂g already works (2, 4, 17, 65), and so does the determinant of the metric (5, 64, 66), Ricci as a declared contraction (13, 63), prove with hypotheses written with indices (10, 14, 15, 68, 69), counting independent components (12, 13, 61), coordinates with indices (62), linearized theory (71), and the transformation law of the Christoffel symbols, with both charts on the same index type (3). In components, Sucuri computes Christoffel, Riemann, Ricci and the scalar of any metric — given by its diagonal, by the line element, or induced by an embedding or a change of coordinates —, geodesics (the equations, what is conserved, and the orbits by quadrature), the orthonormal basis with Cartan's forms, and fields by components — ∇A, the Laplacian, ℒXg and the bracket —; and in_chart evaluates any indexed expression component by component (36, 78). What is missing is argument, or finding a transformation.

15. Conserved current of a Killing field solves

Problem 15 · Conserved current associated with a Killing vector

H. S. Reall, Part III General Relativity (2022), §5.3 “Lie derivative”, last exercise of the section, p. 57 — damtp.cam.ac.uk; and D. Tong, General Relativity: Example Sheet 3 (2019), question 5, p. 2 — davidtong.org.

Problem 52 · Contact forms on 3-manifolds

C. Wendl, Differential Geometry I, Problem Set 12 (Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin, 2016–17), problem 4, parts (a)–(d), pp. 3–4 — mathematik.hu-berlin.de.

4. Suppose M is an oriented 3-manifold and λ ∈ Ω1(M) is nowhere zero, i.e. for all p ∈ M there exist vectors X ∈ TpM with λ(X) ≠ 0. Then at every p ∈ M, the kernel ker λp = {X ∈ TpM | λ(X) = 0} is a 2-dimensional subspace of TpM, and the union of these for all p defines a smooth 2-dimensional distribution

ξ := ker λ ⊂ TM.

(a) Show that the following conditions are equivalent:
i. λ ∧ dλ ≡ 0;
ii. For all p ∈ M and X, Y ∈ TpM, dλ(X, Y) = 0 [sic; from context, read X, Y ∈ ξp];
iii. ξ is integrable.
Hint: In Problem Set 6 #4(c), you will find a useful formula for dλ as a C∞-bilinear form on vector fields. Combine this with the Frobenius theorem.

The 1-form λ is called a contact form if λ ∧ dλ is a volume form; the distribution ξ (called the contact structure) is then “as non-integrable as possible.” An example on ℝ3 is shown in the figure in the original. Such examples can be constructed by the following trick. Let (ρ, φ, z) denote the standard cylindrical coordinates on ℝ3, so x = ρ cos φ and y = ρ sin φ. Choose smooth real-valued functions f(ρ), g(ρ) and define λ at (ρ, φ, z) by

λ = f(ρ) dz + g(ρ) dφ.   (2)

(b) Since the coordinates (ρ, φ, z) are not well defined at ρ = 0, there is of course some danger that the 1-form defined in Equation (2) might be singular at the z-axis. Show that λ is in fact smooth on all of ℝ3 and satisfies λ ∧ dλ ≠ 0 near the z-axis if we assume f(ρ) = 1 and g(ρ) = ρ2 for ρ sufficiently close to 0. Hint: Convert to Cartesian coordinates.

(c) Assuming f and g take the form described above for ρ near 0, show that λ is a contact form if and only if

f(ρ)g′(ρ) − f′(ρ)g(ρ) ≠ 0

for all ρ > 0. What does this mean geometrically about the curve ρ ↦ (f(ρ), g(ρ)) ∈ ℝ2? Interpret this in terms of “twisting” of the planes ξp as p ∈ ℝ3 moves along radial paths away from the z-axis.

(d) By a fundamental result in contact geometry known as Gray's theorem, contact structures have the following remarkable “stability” property: if M is a closed 3-manifold and {ξt}t∈[0,1] is any smooth family of contact structures on M, then they are all “equivalent” in the sense that there exists a smooth family of diffeomorphisms {ϕt : M → M}t∈[0,1] such that ϕ0 = Id and Tϕt(ξ0) = ξt for all t ∈ [0, 1]. Show that this is not true in general for arbitrary smooth families of distributions, e.g. it becomes false if we assume that ξ0 is integrable but ξt is a contact structure for each t > 0.

In Sucuri, partly: (c): λ∧dλ = (f g′ − f′ g) dρ∧dφ∧dz, and λ is contact if and only if that coefficient does not vanish; the equivalences in (a), the regularity on the axis in (b) and the stability in (d), no.

x = coordinates(\rho, \phi, z) f = f(\rho) h = h(\rho) \lambda = form(f dz + h d\phi) D = exterior(\lambda) wedge(\lambda, D) (f(rho)*Derivative(h(rho), rho) - h(rho)*Derivative(f(rho), rho))*drho∧dphi∧dz

with Wendl's g renamed h, because g is the metric.

V. What does not come out

Saying this is part of the job. Each item below is an exercise from the problem sets, and the reason is what the program lacks — not the exercise.

Those that come out only partly because the problem asks for more than the central computation — geodesics, embeddings, volumes, parallel transport — are not listed here one by one: the note under each problem statement says what is left out.

No exercise in the list comes out with nothing. What the program lacks, in the partial ones, is this:

  • Arguments, not computations. Existence and smoothness (34), the flow of a vector field (26, 47), "two derivations that agree on functions and on df agree on everything" (46), adding up infinitesimal rotations (36), the invariance of distance under isometries (81): Sucuri checks steps, and does not write the argument.
  • The general lemma. εε for general n and p (16), the closed formula for the count (61), p-forms of arbitrary degree (27, 28): Sucuri proves it in each dimension and degree, not by induction.
  • Pull-back, and ⋆d⋆ in terms of ∇ (31, 33).
  • Change of chart in a given metric (39). The transformation law comes out (3); finding the flat coordinates is trial and error.
  • Drawings and discussions (52, 65, 77): what the problem asks for beyond the computation.

VI. What the exercises taught

A notation reader can only be tested with notation written by other people, for another purpose. Working through these problem sets found sixteen bugs — eight of them silent, the kind of error this program exists not to make. All fixed, each with a test.

  • The vanishing ½ (exercise 1). T(ab)X[a|cd|b] simplified to 2·T·X + 2·T·X. In ½(X − Y), SymPy keeps the ½ as an argument of the product, not in the coefficient; reading only the coefficient, the ½ and the sign were lost. A wrong answer, with no warning.
  • The tensor that became a function (exercise 15). T^{ab} (…) was read as T applied to the parenthesis. The safeguard caught it — the internal markers leaked, and the program refused —, but the legitimate reading did not get through.
  • The index that became a question. A^a (B_a + C_a) asked whether the index a was a function.
  • The Greek name (exercise 39). \phi = \phi(x) was not a declaration; φ stayed constant, and the metric −e2φdt² + e−2ψdx² came out flat, R = 0.
  • The diffgeom field (exercises 39, 40, 45). The derivative of a function of the coordinate came out as Subs(…), with the library's scalar field in place of the coordinate — correct, unreadable, and with an internal object leaking into what gets copied.
  • One metric inside another (exercise 45). After computing FRW with k, in (t, r, θ, φ), flat FRW, in (t, x, y, z), came out with an object from the first chart in the middle of the result: SymPy's cache returned, in the computation of one metric, pieces of the computation of another. It only showed up because the suite runs the exercises in sequence.
  • The Greek argument (exercise 73). F = F(\varpi) stored the variable as \varpi, with the backslash — a different symbol from the coordinate. F stayed constant, and the surface of revolution, flat.
  • The hidden εε pair (exercise 59). In ε × (sum), the identity did not find the pair inside each term; and SymPy swaps the dummy indices around when building a product, so εijkεklm arrived with its indices scrambled. With the metric declared, it now aligns them.
  • The equality simplified raw (exercise 59). The difference of the two sides was simplified before each side: identities that closed side by side did not close together.
  • ∂ of the symbol (exercise 51). The Levi-Civita symbol is ±1 in every chart, and its ∂ is zero — which Sucuri did not know.
  • ∂ that went through the metric (exercises 59 and 66). ∂μ(∂μφ) is ∂μ(gμν∂νφ), and the metric is inside the derivative; Sucuri put it outside, and the ∂g term silently vanished. In a Cartesian chart ∂g = 0 and the answer agreed — which is why 59 came out —, but the chart had not been stated. Now ∂ differentiates the index in its declared position, with g explicit, and the Cartesian chart is a declaration: g = metric(cartesiana). The same bug read gαβ∂gαβ, with an index raised by the contraction, as ∂δ = 0.
  • The Γ on a contracted pair (exercise 71). expand of ∇δ(∇αVα) put in a Γ for each index of ∇αVα — including the two α, which are dummies: the operand is a scalar. The sum of the spurious terms cancelled, but the indices did not close, and SymPy refused.
  • The dummy with the derivative's name (exercise 10). SymPy names the dummies of a product L0, L1…, and the index of an outer derivative could have the same name; differentiating factor by factor, the dummy looked free and repeated.
  • Lowering an index on an upper key (exercise 12). When replacing tensors by components, SymPy lowers indices wrongly starting from an array of upper indices, with a non-diagonal metric: the Bianchi identity, which is true, gave nonzero equations, and the most general Riemann came out zero — and every identity came out true. The arrays now have lower indices, and raising SymPy does correctly.
  • The division by d − 2 (exercise 25). Schur's lemma comes out by dividing by d − 2, and the proof said "proved" without saying that in 2D it does not hold. Now a symbolic denominator becomes a condition, stated in the answer.
  • The colliding dummy (exercise 34). Matching the free index of a hypothesis to a dummy of the goal, in a hypothesis that has its own dummy inside — ωiaXaj = δij —, gave two equal indices, and the candidate was silently discarded: ωiaXalVi = Vl could not be proved. It was not a wrong answer, but a proof that existed and was not found. The goal's dummies now get fresh names before matching.
